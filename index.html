    Introduction to Substations

    A substation is a node of the power system where voltage is transformed, circuits are switched and protected, and power flow is measured and controlled. Every kilowatt-hour you use has passed through several of them.

    What is a substation?

    A substation is an assembly of high-voltage equipment — transformers, circuit breakers, disconnectors, busbars, instrument transformers, surge arresters and their protection, control and communication systems — installed at one site to connect, convert and protect parts of the electrical network. Substations sit between generation, transmission lines and distribution feeders, acting as the junctions where power is redirected, stepped up or down, and isolated when something goes wrong.

    Mental modelThink of a substation as a highway interchange for electricity: the busbars are the roads, circuit breakers are the gates that can close instantly in an emergency, transformers are the ramps between highways of different “speeds” (voltages), and the control room is the traffic management centre.

    Where substations fit in the power system

    Power is generated at medium voltage, stepped up to transmission voltage to cut I²R losses over distance, then stepped down in stages. Each step-down point is a substation. Higher voltage means lower current for the same power (P = √3·V·I·cosφ), so smaller conductors and much lower losses.

    Philippine contextThe Philippine transmission grid uses 500, 230, 138, 115 and 69 kV, while distribution utilities and electric cooperatives commonly use 34.5, 13.8 and 13.2 kV primary feeders (and 7.62–7.97 kV phase-to-neutral on single-phase laterals), with 230 V secondary service. Always confirm against your utility's standards.

    Core functions of a substation

    Voltage transformation

    Step voltage up or down with power transformers so energy can move efficiently and be used safely.

    Switching

    Connect and disconnect lines, transformers and loads for operation, maintenance and reconfiguration.

    Protection

    Detect faults with relays and isolate them in milliseconds with circuit breakers to protect people, equipment and system stability.

    Voltage & reactive control

    Tap changers, capacitor banks, reactors, SVCs and STATCOMs keep voltage within limits.

    Measurement & metering

    Instrument transformers feed meters, relays and SCADA with scaled-down currents and voltages.

    Interconnection

    Tie together lines, generating plants and neighbouring grids so power can flow where it's needed.

    Conversion

    Converter stations change AC to DC (HVDC) or connect asynchronous grids; traction substations feed railways.

    Communication hub

    RTUs, gateways, fibre and PLCC carry data and teleprotection signals to the control centre and remote ends.

    Anatomy of a typical substation

    Walking through an outdoor HV substation you will typically find:

    • Switchyard – outdoor area containing the HV equipment arranged in bays.
    • Transformer area – power transformers on foundations with oil containment pits, fire walls and sometimes deluge systems.
    • Control building / relay house – protection and control panels, SCADA, communication equipment, battery room, AC/DC distribution boards.
    • MV switchgear room – indoor metal-clad switchgear for outgoing distribution feeders.
    • Cable trenches and ducts – carrying control and power cables between yard and building.
    • Grounding grid – a buried mesh of copper conductors under the entire site.
    • Lightning protection – shield wires and/or masts over the yard.
    • Perimeter fence, gates, lighting, access roads – security and safe access for heavy equipment.

    The “bay” concept

    A bay is the set of equipment that connects one circuit (a line, a transformer, a capacitor bank, a bus coupler) to the busbars. Substations are designed, built, numbered and operated bay-by-bay. Common bay types: Line bayTransformer bayBus coupler / bus section bayCapacitor / reactor bayFeeder bayGenerator bay

    Why this order? The arrester is first so it meets incoming surges before anything else. The CVT and wave trap serve line metering, protection and power-line-carrier communication. The line disconnector with earth switch provides visible isolation and lets crews ground the line. CTs sit next to the breaker so the protection zones overlap at the breaker, and the bus disconnector isolates the breaker from the bus for maintenance.

    Single-Line Diagrams & Bus Schemes

    The bus scheme — how breakers and buses are connected — decides how reliable, flexible, maintainable and expensive a substation is. It is the most important early design decision.

    Reading a single-line diagram (SLD)

    A single-line diagram represents a three-phase system with one line per circuit. It shows the electrical connections, equipment ratings, CT/VT ratios and protection functions. Every operator and engineer must be fluent in it. Common symbols:

    Know-howWhen reading an SLD, trace a fault: “If a fault happens here, which breakers must open, which relays see it, and what loses supply?” Doing this for each scheme is the fastest way to understand its strengths and weaknesses.

    Single bus

    All circuits connect to one bus. Simple, cheap and easy to operate and protect — but a bus fault or bus maintenance means a total outage, and breaker maintenance takes that circuit out of service. Sectionalizing with a bus-tie breaker splits the bus so a fault only drops one half; supplying each half from a separate source gives reasonable reliability at modest cost. This is the workhorse of distribution substations.

    Main and transfer bus

    Adds a transfer bus and a bus coupler (transfer) breaker. To maintain a feeder breaker, the operator closes the coupler, closes that feeder's bypass disconnector, then opens and isolates the feeder breaker — the circuit stays energised through the coupler, which now also carries that feeder's protection (via relay transfer switching). Still vulnerable to a main bus fault, and only one breaker can be bypassed at a time.

    Double bus, single breaker

    Each circuit has one breaker and two bus-selector disconnectors. Circuits can be split between buses to limit fault level or separate sources, and either bus can be taken out for maintenance by transferring circuits to the other (with the coupler closed, the selectors can be switched make-before-break). A bus fault drops only the circuits on that bus. Weakness: breaker maintenance still needs a circuit outage unless a bypass is added; a coupler breaker failure can drop both buses. Common in HV transmission and large industrial plants.

    Double bus, double breaker

    Every circuit has two breakers, one to each bus. Any breaker or either bus can be taken out with no circuit outage; a bus fault loses no circuits; a breaker failure affects only one circuit. Used for critical EHV substations and large generating stations where cost is justified.

    Breaker-and-a-half

    Each diameter has three breakers in series between two main buses, with two circuits connected between them — so 1½ breakers per circuit. Any breaker can be maintained without an outage, a bus fault drops no circuits, and it is easy to expand. The centre (tie) breaker is shared, so protection and reclosing logic are more complex, and a tie-breaker failure can drop both circuits in that diameter. It is the standard for EHV transmission substations in many countries. Good practice: place a source and a load circuit in the same diameter, and never two circuits that back each other up.

    Ring bus

    One breaker per circuit, yet every circuit is fed from two sides. Any breaker can be maintained without an outage and there are no main buses to fail. But when the ring is open for maintenance, a second fault can split the substation, the ring is hard to expand beyond about six circuits, and relaying/reclosing is more complex. Popular for HV substations with four to six circuits and as the first stage of a future breaker-and-a-half.

    How to choose

    1. Define the consequence of outage: how much load or generation is lost, and for how long, for each credible contingency.
    2. Apply the planning criteria (e.g., N-1, N-1-1) and grid code requirements.
    3. Consider maintainability: can breakers be maintained without outages?
    4. Allow for future expansion — e.g., build a ring bus laid out to convert to breaker-and-a-half.
    5. Compare life-cycle cost (capital + outage cost + maintenance) and available land.
    6. Check protection complexity and operator familiarity.

    Power Transformers

    The power transformer is the most expensive and most critical item in most substations. Lead times can exceed a year, so understanding, protecting and maintaining it is a core substation skill.

    Operating principle

    An alternating current in the primary winding creates an alternating magnetic flux in the laminated steel core, which induces a voltage in the secondary winding. The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio, and the current ratio is its inverse.

    Transformer equationsV₁ / V₂ = N₁ / N₂ = I₂ / I₁ E = 4.44 · f · N · Φmax (EMF per winding, volts) I_rated = S / (√3 · V_LL) (three-phase rated current)

    Construction

    Core

    Cold-rolled grain-oriented (CRGO) silicon steel laminations to minimise eddy and hysteresis losses. Core-type (3- or 5-limb) is most common; shell-type is used for some very large units. The core is grounded at one point only.

    Windings

    Copper (sometimes aluminium) conductors, paper-insulated. Disc, helical and layer windings; LV usually nearest the core, HV outside. Continuously transposed conductor (CTC) for large currents.

    Insulation system

    Oil-impregnated paper and pressboard. Liquid: mineral oil, or natural/synthetic ester (higher fire point, biodegradable). Paper ageing ultimately sets transformer life.

    Tank & cooling

    Welded steel tank with radiators, fans, oil pumps or water coolers depending on rating.

    Bushings

    Bring conductors through the tank. HV bushings are condenser type (OIP, RIP or RIS) with a test tap for capacitance/tan δ measurement.

    Conservator & breather

    Expansion tank lets oil expand with temperature. A silica-gel breather (or rubber bag/diaphragm) keeps moisture out; change the gel when about two-thirds has changed colour.

    Tap changers and voltage regulation

    • De-energised tap changer (DETC / off-circuit) – changed only with the transformer de-energised; typically ±2 × 2.5%. Used for seasonal or one-time adjustment.
    • On-load tap changer (OLTC) – changes taps under load using a diverter switch with transition resistors (or reactors in North American designs) so the current is never interrupted. Typical range ±10% in 16 steps of 1.25% (17 positions). Modern OLTCs use vacuum interrupters, greatly extending maintenance intervals.
    • Automatic voltage regulator (AVR, ANSI 90) – controls the OLTC to keep the bus voltage within a deadband, with a time delay to avoid hunting; line drop compensation (LDC) can regulate voltage at a remote load point.
    Know-howOLTC problems are a leading cause of transformer failures. Track the operations counter, check for “tap position disagreement” between parallel units, and maintain the diverter by operations count or time per the manufacturer.

    Vector groups

    The vector group describes winding connections and phase displacement. In Dyn11: D = HV delta, y = LV star (wye), n = LV neutral brought out, 11 = LV leads HV by 30° (clock position 11 × 30° = 330° lag = 30° lead).

    Parallel operation

    Two transformers can share load in parallel only if:

    1. Same voltage ratio (and tap position) — otherwise a circulating current flows.
    2. Same vector group / phase displacement — mandatory; a 30° error causes a dead short.
    3. Same polarity and phase sequence.
    4. Similar percent impedance (within about ±10%) and similar X/R — otherwise load divides unequally (the lower-impedance unit takes more).
    5. Preferably a rating ratio not exceeding about 3:1.

    Losses and efficiency

    • No-load (core / iron) losses – hysteresis and eddy currents; present whenever energised, independent of load.
    • Load (copper) losses – I²R plus stray and eddy losses in windings and structure; proportional to load squared.
    • Efficiency is maximum when load losses equal no-load losses. Power transformers achieve 99.5%+ efficiency.

    Reading a nameplate

    Key nameplate data: rated power per cooling stage, rated voltages and tap range, rated currents, frequency, vector group, impedance voltage (%Z) at principal tap, insulation levels (LI/AC per terminal), cooling class, oil volume and type, total and transport mass, serial number and year, and the connection diagram. Always photograph and record nameplates during commissioning.

    Special transformers

    Autotransformer

    Shares part of the winding between HV and LV. Smaller, cheaper and more efficient for ratios under about 3:1 (e.g., 500/230 kV, 230/115 kV). No galvanic isolation; higher fault currents.

    Grounding transformer

    Zigzag or YNd unit that provides a ground source on an ungrounded/delta system.

    Station service transformer

    Supplies the substation's own LV AC loads. Sometimes a tertiary winding or an SSVT (station service voltage transformer) is used.

    Phase-shifting transformer

    Controls active power flow between parallel paths by adjusting phase angle.

    Switchgear: Breakers, Disconnectors & Interlocks

    Switchgear makes and breaks circuits. Circuit breakers interrupt load and fault currents; disconnectors provide visible isolation; earth switches make equipment safe to touch.

    Circuit breakers — how they interrupt current

    When breaker contacts part, an arc forms and current continues to flow through the ionised gap. AC current naturally passes through zero twice per cycle. The breaker's job is to cool and de-ionise the gap so quickly at current zero that the arc cannot re-strike against the transient recovery voltage (TRV) that appears across the contacts. Total break time for HV breakers is typically 2–3 cycles (≈ 33–50 ms at 60 Hz).

    Reclosers and sectionalizers

    A recloser is a self-contained breaker with its own relay that trips and recloses automatically (typically up to 3–4 attempts) to clear temporary faults on overhead distribution lines — most faults (often cited as 70–90%) on overhead lines are temporary (lightning, branches, birds). A sectionalizer counts recloser operations and opens during a dead interval to isolate a permanently faulted section; it cannot interrupt fault current itself.

    Disconnectors (isolators)

    Disconnectors provide a visible, reliable isolation gap. They are not designed to interrupt load or fault current — only small charging or magnetizing currents (and bus-transfer current under defined conditions). Opening a disconnector on load draws a dangerous arc.

    Earthing (grounding) switches are usually mounted on the disconnector frame and connect isolated conductors to earth. Earth switches on lines may need induced-current switching capability where parallel energised lines induce voltage.

    Load-break switches (common at MV) can interrupt load current but not fault current; combined with fuses they form a cheap protected switch for ring main units (RMUs).

    Interlocking

    Interlocks prevent dangerous operating sequences. Classic rules:

    • A disconnector can operate only when its associated breaker is open.
    • An earth switch can close only when the associated disconnectors are open (and the circuit is dead).
    • A disconnector cannot close while its earth switch is closed.
    • Bus-selector disconnectors in double-bus schemes can transfer on-load only when the bus coupler is closed.

    Implementations: mechanical (linkages), electrical (hard-wired auxiliary contacts in control circuits), key / trapped-key systems (e.g., Castell keys), and software interlocking in bay controllers via IEC 61850 GOOSE. Defeating an interlock should require written authorisation.

    Gas-insulated switchgear (GIS) internals

    • Enclosures – aluminium or steel, grounded, single-phase (higher voltages) or three-phase-in-one (lower HV).
    • Gas compartments – separated by gas-tight cast-resin barrier insulators so a fault or maintenance affects one compartment only. Each has a density monitor.
    • Three-position switch – combined disconnector/earth switch (closed – open – earthed) prevents incompatible positions mechanically.
    • Fast-acting earth switch – fault-making rated, used on line entries.
    • Bellows / expansion joints – absorb thermal expansion and tolerances.
    • Interfaces – SF₆-air bushings, cable terminations, direct transformer connections.
    • Monitoring – UHF partial discharge sensors, gas density trends.
    VFTODisconnector operations in GIS generate very fast transient overvoltages (VFTO) with rise times of nanoseconds. These can stress transformer windings and secondary systems — good grounding and shielding are essential.

    MV metal-clad switchgear

    Indoor MV distribution uses metal-enclosed switchboards with separate compartments for the breaker, busbars, cables and LV control. Key concepts:

    • Withdrawable breakers (truck-type) give visible isolation in the “test” or “disconnected” position.
    • Loss of Service Continuity category (IEC 62271-200: LSC1, LSC2, LSC2A, LSC2B) describes which compartments stay live when one is opened.
    • Internal Arc Classification (IAC) e.g., IAC AFLR 31.5 kA 1 s — tested to protect personnel on front, lateral and rear sides from arc fault effects. Arc ducts vent gases away.
    • Ring main units (RMUs) – compact SF₆ or solid/air-insulated switchgear for secondary distribution loops.

    Other Primary Equipment

    Beyond transformers and switchgear, a substation relies on instrument transformers, surge arresters, insulators, conductors and reactive-power equipment.

    Current transformers (CTs)

    CTs scale primary current down to a standard secondary value — 1 A (common for long cable runs, lower burden) or 5 A — for relays and meters, and isolate secondary circuits from HV.

    • Ratio e.g., 1200/1 A. Multi-ratio CTs have taps; protection CTs often have several cores (one per protection/metering function).
    • Polarity – P1/P2 (primary) and S1/S2 (secondary). Wrong polarity makes differential and directional relays operate incorrectly.
    • Burden – load on the CT secondary in VA (or ohms): relay + leads. Long leads increase burden.
    • Knee-point voltage – the point where a 10% increase in voltage causes a 50% increase in excitation current. Above it the CT saturates.

    Voltage transformers (VTs / PTs) and CVTs

    • Inductive (magnetic) VT – wound transformer. Standard secondary 110 V or 110/√3 V (IEC); 120 V / 69.3 V (IEEE). Common up to ~145 kV.
    • Capacitor voltage transformer (CVT / CCVT) – capacitive divider plus intermediate transformer. Cheaper at HV/EHV and doubles as a coupling capacitor for PLCC. Has transient response that distance relays must account for.
    • Accuracy: 0.2, 0.5, 1.0 metering; 3P, 6P protection.
    • Ferroresonance – inductive VTs on ungrounded or lightly loaded systems can enter a sustained nonlinear oscillation causing overvoltage and overheating. Damping resistors on open-delta windings help.
    Never short-circuit a VT secondaryA VT behaves like a voltage source; shorting the secondary causes a huge current that destroys it. Secondary circuits are protected by fuses or MCBs, and VT-fuse-failure supervision blocks distance protection when the voltage is lost.

    Non-conventional instrument transformers (NCITs) – optical CTs, Rogowski coils and resistive/capacitive dividers with digital output via merging units are used in digital substations (IEC 61869 series).

    Porcelain

    Proven, rigid, UV-proof. Heavy and brittle; contamination washes off by rain only partially.

    Busbars and conductors

    Line trap and PLCC

    Power-line carrier communication (PLCC) sends signals (≈ 30–500 kHz) along the HV line for teleprotection and voice/data. The wave trap (line trap) — a parallel LC circuit in series with the line — blocks carrier frequencies from entering the substation bus while passing 50/60 Hz power.     Protection Systems

    Protection detects abnormal conditions and isolates the smallest possible part of the system, fast enough to limit damage and keep the grid stable.

    Principles of good protection

    Selectivity

    Trip only the breakers needed to isolate the fault — nothing more.

    Speed

    Clear faults quickly — EHV main protection often clears within ~80–100 ms total — to limit damage and preserve stability.

    Sensitivity

    Detect the smallest fault (e.g., high-resistance earth faults) within the zone.

    Reliability

    Dependability (always trips for in-zone faults) and security (never trips for out-of-zone faults or normal conditions).

    Simplicity & economy

    Proportionate to the value and criticality of what is protected.

    Zones of protection

    The system is divided into overlapping zones: generator, transformer, bus, line, feeder, motor, capacitor. Zones are bounded by CTs and must overlap around each circuit breaker so no point is unprotected. That's why CTs are placed on both sides of the breaker, or why multi-core CTs are wired so that bus protection uses the core on the line side and line protection uses the core on the bus side.

    Main and backup: Every element has main protection and backup. Backup can be local (a second relay or breaker-failure protection at the same substation) or remote (time-delayed protection at the adjacent substation). EHV systems typically use duplicated Main 1 / Main 2 protection from different manufacturers or principles, with separate CT cores, DC supplies and trip coils.

    Overcurrent protection and coordination

    Overcurrent relays operate when current exceeds a pickup (setting) Is. Inverse definite minimum time (IDMT) relays trip faster for larger currents. IEC 60255 standard curves:

    IEC IDMT characteristics (M = I / Is, TMS = time multiplier)Standard inverse (SI): t = TMS × 0.14 / (M^0.02 − 1) Very inverse (VI): t = TMS × 13.5 / (M − 1) Extremely inverse (EI): t = TMS × 80 / (M² − 1) Long-time inverse: t = TMS × 120 / (M − 1)

    IEEE C37.112 defines similar Moderately/Very/Extremely Inverse curves. Coordination rules of thumb:

    • Pickup above maximum load current (often 1.2–1.5 × full load) and below minimum fault current at the end of the zone (including backup reach).
    • Coordination time interval (CTI) between upstream and downstream relays: typically 0.2–0.4 s (≈ 0.3 s with numerical relays and fast breakers) — covers breaker clearing time, relay overtravel and errors.
    • Choose the same curve family along a radial path; use VI/EI curves where fault current varies strongly with location and to coordinate with fuses.
    • Instantaneous elements (50) set above maximum through-fault for the next downstream device.
    • Earth fault settings can be much lower than phase settings because normal residual current is small (typ. 10–40% of CT rating).

    Distance protection (21)

    A distance relay measures the impedance Z = V / I seen from the relay. Because line impedance is proportional to length, the relay can tell how far away the fault is. Characteristics: mho, quadrilateral (better resistive reach for earth faults), offset mho.

    Zone 2≥120% of protected line (or 100% + 50% of shortest adjacent line)0.3–0.5 sCovers remaining 15–20% and remote bus

    Teleprotection schemes make the end-of-line 15–20% clear instantly: DUTT (direct underreach transfer trip), PUTT (permissive underreach), POTT (permissive overreach), DCB (directional comparison blocking), and DTT (direct transfer trip for breaker failure or reactor faults).

    Differential protection (87)

    Kirchhoff's law: in a healthy zone, current in equals current out. A differential relay compares currents at all zone boundaries; any significant difference means an internal fault. It is inherently selective and fast.

    • Biased (percentage) characteristic – operating threshold rises with through current to tolerate CT errors and tap-changer mismatch.
    • Transformer specifics – numerical relays compensate for ratio and vector-group phase shift internally; 2nd-harmonic restraint blocks on magnetising inrush; 5th-harmonic restraint blocks on overexcitation; an unrestrained high-set element trips heavy internal faults regardless.
    • High-impedance bus differential – uses a stabilising resistor so a saturated CT on an external fault doesn't cause a trip; requires identical-ratio class PX CTs.
    • Line differential (87L) – exchanges current samples over fibre/communication channels; requires channel delay compensation (GPS or ping-pong).
    • Restricted earth fault (REF) – sensitive protection for earth faults near the neutral end of a star winding that normal differential may miss.

    Breaker failure protection (50BF)

    If a breaker receives a trip but current keeps flowing after a short timer (typically 100–200 ms; retrip of own breaker first), breaker-failure protection trips all adjacent breakers that can feed the fault — every breaker on the same bus section, plus a direct transfer trip to the remote line end.

    Auto-reclosing (79) and synchro-check (25)

    Most overhead-line faults are transient. After a trip, the breaker recloses after a dead time (long enough for the arc path to de-ionise — roughly 0.3–0.5 s or more at HV) to restore supply. Single-pole reclosing keeps two phases connected during single-phase faults to maintain stability. Synchro-check (25) verifies voltage, angle and frequency difference before closing between two live systems. Reclosing is normally blocked for cable circuits, transformer faults, breaker failure and busbar faults.

    Trip circuits and DC

    • Protection trips through lockout relays (86) for transformer and bus faults — the breaker cannot be re-closed until the lockout is manually reset after investigation.
    • Trip circuit supervision (74) continuously checks the trip coil circuit continuity.
    • Redundant DC supplies and dual trip coils for transmission breakers.
    • Relay technology evolved from electromechanical → static (solid-state) → numerical IEDs with self-monitoring, event records, disturbance recording (COMTRADE files) and communication.
    Know-howAfter any trip, download and save the relay event and disturbance records before resetting or making setting changes. The waveforms tell you the fault type, phase, location, magnitude and whether the protection behaved correctly.

    Grounding & Lightning Protection

    A good grounding system keeps people safe during faults, provides a low-impedance path for fault current, and lets protective relays operate correctly.

    Purposes of substation grounding

    • Limit touch and step voltages to safe values during ground faults (personnel safety).
    • Provide a low-impedance return path so relays detect and clear faults.
    • Hold equipment frames at a common potential (equipotential bonding).
    • Discharge lightning and switching surges via arresters and shield wires.
    • Provide a reference for the system neutral and for electronic equipment.

    Key voltages during a ground fault

    TermDefinition
    GPR (Ground Potential Rise)Grid current × grid resistance: the voltage of the whole grid relative to remote earth
    Touch voltageDifference between a grounded structure a person touches and the earth surface where they stand (hand-to-feet)
    Step voltageDifference across 1 m of earth surface between a person's feet
    Mesh voltageMaximum touch voltage within a grid mesh (usually the design limiting case)
    Transferred voltageGPR carried out of the substation by metallic objects (fences, cables, pipes, rails) to remote locations — a special hazard

    Tolerable touch and step voltages (IEEE 80)

    IEEE Std 80 relates tolerable body current to shock duration and calculates the voltage a person can safely withstand, accounting for a high-resistivity surface layer (crushed rock):

    Tolerable limits (ts = fault duration in s, ρs = surface layer resistivity Ω·m, Cs = surface derating factor)E_step,50kg = (1000 + 6·Cs·ρs) × 0.116 / √ts E_touch,50kg = (1000 + 1.5·Cs·ρs) × 0.116 / √ts E_step,70kg = (1000 + 6·Cs·ρs) × 0.157 / √ts E_touch,70kg = (1000 + 1.5·Cs·ρs) × 0.157 / √ts

    The 1000 Ω term is the assumed body resistance. Notice how strongly the surface layer helps: a 100–150 mm layer of crushed rock (ρs ≈ 2000–5000 Ω·m when wet) raises tolerable touch voltage several-fold compared with bare soil.

    Soil resistivity measurement — Wenner method

    Four equally spaced electrodes (spacing a) are driven in a straight line. Current is injected through the outer pair and voltage measured across the inner pair. The apparent resistivity is:

    ρ = 2π · a · R (valid when electrode depth b ≪ a)

    Measurements at several spacings (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 m) and in different directions reveal layered soil, which is modelled (two-layer or multi-layer) for grid design software. Measure in the dry season for conservative results.

    Grounding grid design

    1. Collect data: soil resistivity, site area, maximum ground fault current and its split (how much returns through shield wires and cable sheaths versus the grid), fault clearing time.
    2. Size conductors for thermal withstand (IEEE 80 equation; see Chapter 17) plus mechanical robustness and corrosion allowance.
    3. Calculate tolerable touch and step voltages with the chosen surface layer.
    4. Initial layout: perimeter loop enclosing all equipment, parallel conductors forming meshes (often 3–10 m spacing), buried 0.5–0.8 m deep, plus ground rods especially along the perimeter and at arresters.
    5. Calculate grid resistance and GPR. If GPR is below tolerable touch voltage, the design is safe; otherwise compute mesh and step voltages.
    6. Refine: closer mesh spacing at corners/perimeter, more rods, deeper conductors, or extend the grid until mesh and step voltages are below tolerable limits.
    7. Check transferred voltages, fence grounding, and connections to external systems.
    Approximate grid resistance (Laurent–Niemann, IEEE 80)Rg ≈ (ρ / 4) · √(π / A) + ρ / L_T A = grid area (m²), L_T = total buried conductor length (m)

    Typical targets: around 1 Ω or less for large transmission substations, and a few ohms for smaller distribution substations — but safety is proven by touch and step voltage, not by resistance alone.

    Equipment grounding practice

    • Bond every metallic structure, equipment tank and frame — critical equipment (transformers, breakers, arresters) with two separate connections to the grid.
    • Use exothermic welds or listed compression connectors below grade; bolted connections above grade where inspection is possible.
    • Connect arrester and transformer neutral grounds with short, direct leads plus dedicated rods.
    • Fence: either bond to the main grid with a perimeter conductor about 1 m outside the fence, or isolate and ground separately — the choice depends on touch voltage outside the fence.
    • Cable trays, gates (with flexible bonds across hinges), and building steel are all bonded.
    • Ground secondary CT/VT circuits at one point only to avoid circulating currents.

    Measuring grid resistance — fall-of-potential

    Inject current between the grid and a remote current probe; measure voltage between the grid and a potential probe moved along the line. For a uniform soil and a hemispherical electrode, the true resistance is read with the potential probe at about 61.8% (“62% rule”) of the distance to the current probe. For large grids, the current probe must be very far away (several times the grid diagonal), so methods like the slope method, injected-current test with high current, or clamp-on methods for individual electrodes are also used.

    Lightning (direct-stroke) protection

    Overhead shield wires and/or lightning masts intercept strokes before they reach energised conductors. Design methods:

    • Fixed angle – classic empirical method; protected zone defined by 30°–45° from vertical from the shield wire or mast (lower angles for taller structures).
    • Rolling sphere – an imaginary sphere of radius S rolled over the site; anything the sphere touches is exposed. IEEE 998 relates striking distance to stroke current:
    IEEE 998 electrogeometric modelS = 8 · k · I^0.65 (S in m, I in kA; k = 1 for wires, 1.2 for masts) Is = 2.2 · BIL / Z₀ (critical stroke current that just causes flashover)

    Shielding failures (strokes that slip past) are allowed only at currents low enough that the arresters and insulation can handle them.

    Surge (overvoltage) protection

    Surges entering on lines are handled by surge arresters at line entries and close to transformers, plus line shielding for the first 1–2 km (sometimes called the “approach span” protection) to reduce the steepness and magnitude of incoming surges. Insulation coordination is covered in Chapter 10.

    Ground system maintenance

    • Visual inspection of above-grade connections and jumpers (theft of copper is a real problem in many places).
    • Point-to-point continuity tests between equipment and the grid (low milliohm values expected).
    • Periodic grid resistance or injection testing, especially after site expansion or rising fault levels.
    • Excavate and inspect sample connections for corrosion in aggressive soils.
    • Maintain the crushed-rock surface layer — weeds and soil contamination reduce its resistivity.

    Substation Design

    Substation design turns a planning need (“we need 2 × 50 MVA at 69/13.8 kV here”) into a buildable, safe, maintainable facility. It is multidisciplinary: electrical, civil, structural, protection, telecom and environmental.

    The design process

    1. Requirements & scope – load forecast, number and rating of transformers and feeders, voltage levels, reliability targets, future expansion, owner standards, grid code connection requirements.
    2. System studies – load flow, short-circuit (max and min fault levels), stability, insulation coordination, grounding, arc-flash, harmonics (if converters or capacitors).
    3. Single-line diagram & bus scheme – choose the scheme (Chapter 3), CT/VT locations and protection philosophy.
    4. Site selection & survey – topographic and geotechnical surveys, soil resistivity, flood level, access.
    5. General arrangement (layout) – plan and section drawings with clearances, bay widths, roads and future bays.
    6. Equipment specifications – technical specs and data sheets for procurement.
    7. Detailed design – grounding, lightning shielding, structures and foundations, cable schedules, protection and control schematics, SCADA, auxiliary AC/DC, lighting, fire protection, drainage, building.
    8. Permits & environment – environmental compliance, noise, EMF, oil spill containment, community consultation.
    9. Procurement, FAT, construction, commissioning – followed by as-built drawings and handover documentation.

    Site selection criteria

    Electrical

    Close to the load centre; easy entry for incoming and outgoing lines; room for future lines.

    Physical

    Level, stable ground; above flood levels (check historical typhoon/storm surge data); good drainage; low soil resistivity is a bonus.

    Access

    Road access for the heaviest load — the power transformer (often 50–200+ t) — including bridges and turning radii.

    Environmental & social

    Distance from homes and schools, noise, visual impact, protected areas, land acquisition and right-of-way.

    Hazards

    Seismic zone, landslides, volcanic hazard, coastal salt pollution, industrial contamination.

    Cost

    Land price, earthworks, line routing cost, and AIS vs GIS trade-off.

    Protection and control testing

    1. Wiring checks – point-to-point continuity and insulation of control wiring against drawings; ferrule and terminal checks.
    2. Secondary injection – inject currents/voltages into each relay with a test set to verify every setting: pickup, timing, characteristics, logic.
    3. Functional / scheme tests – trips to breakers (each trip coil), lockout, breaker failure, reclosing, interlocks, alarms to HMI and SCADA.
    4. End-to-end tests – with GPS-synchronised test sets at both line ends for differential and teleprotection schemes.
    5. Primary injection – inject primary current through CTs to prove ratio, polarity and the entire CT circuit to the relay; stability test for differential protection (through-current, no operation).
    6. SCADA point-to-point – verify every status, alarm, analogue and control with the control centre.
    7. On-load checks – after energisation: phasing, differential stability (spill current near zero), directional checks, metering accuracy.

    Energisation procedure (typical)

    1. All test results reviewed and approved; punch-list items affecting safety closed; settings applied and verified.
    2. Remove all temporary grounds and test leads; account for every one with a register.
    3. Walk-down inspection: foreign objects removed, oil levels, valves open (radiators, Buchholz), gas pressures normal, breather OK.
    4. Issue switching program; all non-essential people out of the yard.
    5. Energise the bus/line and check voltages and phase rotation; then energise the transformer from the HV side with protection in service (expect inrush).
    6. Soak the transformer at no load (commonly 12–24 h); listen for abnormal noise, check temperatures, take a post-energisation DGA sample.
    7. Phase/synchronise with existing systems before paralleling; then apply load in steps.
    8. On-load protection checks, thermography after a few hours/days at load, follow-up DGA (e.g., after 1 week and 1 month).

    Dissolved gas analysis (DGA)

    Faults inside a transformer break down oil and paper into characteristic gases. DGA is the single most valuable transformer condition test.

    Key gasTypical source

    Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆)Low-temperature thermal faults in oil
    Ethylene (C₂H₄)High-temperature thermal faults (> ~300–700 °C) — hot spots, bad joints, circulating currents

    Interpretation methods: key gas method; Rogers ratios and IEC 60599 ratios (C₂H₂/C₂H₄, CH₄/H₂, C₂H₄/C₂H₆); and the Duval triangle, which plots the relative percentages of CH₄, C₂H₄ and C₂H₂ into zones: PD partial dischargeD1 low-energy dischargeD2 high-energy dischargeT1 thermal <300 °CT2 300–700 °CT3 >700 °CDT mixed

    Older IEEE C57.104-2008 “Condition 1” concentrations, still widely quoted as reference points: H₂ 100, CH₄ 120, C₂H₂ 1, C₂H₄ 50, C₂H₆ 65, CO 350, CO₂ 2500 ppm, TDCG 720 ppm. The 2019 revision uses percentile-based limits that depend on transformer age and oxygen content, and it emphasises rates of change over absolute values.

    Know-howTrend matters more than one reading. A transformer with high-but-stable gases may be fine; a modest but rapidly rising gas — especially acetylene or ethylene — demands resampling within days, load reduction, and investigation. Always sample consistently (same valve, flushed, airtight syringe) and note load and temperature.

    Other transformer tasks

    • Check and replace breather silica gel; keep the oil cup filled.
    • Clean radiators and test fans/pumps; verify cooling control stages from WTI/OTI.
    • Inspect bushings for oil leaks, cracks, oil level; test bushing PF/capacitance periodically.
    • OLTC: inspect and service diverter by operations count; test motor drive; filter the OLTC oil (it carbonises).
    • Oil processing: degassing/dehydration, or regeneration (Fuller's earth) for acidic oil; drying the active part if paper moisture is high.
    • Verify protective device operation (Buchholz test via test valve or injection), calibrate thermometers.

    Circuit breaker maintenance

    • Record operation counts and cumulative interrupted current (I²t) — interrupter maintenance is often triggered by these.
    • SF₆: monitor density; find leaks with a leak detector or gas camera; top up with certified gas and handle by recovery carts — never vent.
    • Mechanism: lubricate per manual, check springs, latches, dampers, motor and auxiliary switches; hydraulic oil level and pump run frequency; air compressor and drains on pneumatic types.
    • Electrical tests: timing and travel trending, contact resistance, coil current signatures (first-trip tests capture sticky mechanisms after long idle periods).
    • Exercise breakers that seldom operate — mechanisms that sit for years tend to slow down or stick.
    Industry findingInternational CIGRE reliability surveys have consistently found that operating mechanisms and control/auxiliary circuits cause the majority of HV circuit breaker major failures, rather than the interrupters themselves. Mechanism care pays off.

    Disconnector maintenance

    Disconnector contacts are among the most common hot spots in a yard. Clean and lubricate contacts and pivots with the specified grease, check alignment and contact pressure, check linkages and motor drives, check earth switch contacts and flexible connections, and measure contact resistance. Exercise them periodically.

    Infrared thermography

    Thermal imaging under load finds overheating connections, contacts, unbalanced loading, low oil levels in bushings, and blocked radiators — non-contact and with the equipment in service. Survey at ≥ 40% load where possible; correct for load, emissivity and wind. A commonly used severity table (NETA MTS, temperature rise over a similar component under similar load):

    ΔT over similar componentΔT over ambientAction

    4–15 °C11–20 °CIndicates probable deficiency — repair as time permits
    —21–40 °CMonitor until corrective measures can be taken

    Complementary techniques: ultrasonic (corona, tracking, arcing), UV corona cameras, partial discharge (TEV and airborne acoustic in MV switchgear, UHF in GIS).

    Battery and DC system maintenance

    • Record float voltage at the battery terminals and charger; individual cell voltages periodically.
    • Vented lead-acid: electrolyte level (top up with distilled water), specific gravity (fully charged typically ≈ 1.215–1.250 depending on design), temperature.
    • VRLA: internal ohmic (impedance/conductance) trending — rising impedance indicates dry-out or failure.
    • Clean terminals, check and torque inter-cell connectors, look for corrosion, cracks, swelling.
    • Capacity (discharge) test at installation and periodically (e.g., every few years, more often as the battery ages); replace when capacity falls below ~80% of rated.
    • Test charger alarms, DC earth-fault detection, and battery room ventilation and eyewash.
    • Heat is the enemy: lead-acid life roughly halves for each ~8–10 °C above 25 °C — air-condition battery rooms in tropical climates.

    Protection system maintenance

    Numerical relays self-monitor, but CT/VT circuits, DC supplies, trip paths and communication channels still need periodic verification. Typical programs test protection systems every few years (often 4–6 years for numerical relays), review relay event logs after every operation, keep a version-controlled settings database, and verify settings after firmware updates.

    Yard, civil and housekeeping

    • Vegetation and weed control; keep crushed rock clean and at depth.
    • Drainage clear; oil separators inspected and emptied.
    • Fence integrity, gates, locks, warning signs, animal guards (snakes, rats, birds, monkeys can cause flashovers).
    • Cable trench covers and fire barriers in place; seal cable entries against rodents and water.
    • Control building: air-conditioning, fire alarms and extinguishers, lighting, cleanliness.
    • Record everything in a CMMS (computerised maintenance management system) so trends and histories are available.

    Substation Operations & Switching

    Correct switching is the core operating skill. Most serious substation incidents are switching errors — wrong sequence, wrong bay, or operating a disconnector on load.

    Golden switching rules

    Never operate a disconnector on loadBreakers make and break current; disconnectors only isolate. To de-energise, open the breaker first, then the disconnectors. To energise, close the disconnectors first, then the breaker.

    De-energise (isolate) a bay

    1. Check load can be transferred or interrupted
    2. Open circuit breaker; confirm open (indication + current = 0)
    3. Open line-side disconnector, then bus-side disconnector
    4. Lock and tag; disable breaker controls
    5. Test for absence of voltage
    6. Close earth switches / apply portable grounds

    Re-energise a bay

    1. Confirm work complete, all people clear, permits cancelled
    2. Remove portable grounds; open earth switches
    3. Remove locks and tags
    4. Close bus-side disconnector, then line-side disconnector
    5. Check protection in service and settings
    6. Close circuit breaker (with synchro-check if needed)

    Switching programs and communication

    • Use a written, checked and approved switching program (switching order) for planned work, listing each step in order with the device ID.
    • Three-way communication / repeat-back with the control centre: instruction given, repeated back word-for-word, confirmed, then executed and reported.
    • Verify the correct location, bay and device before operating — read the label, compare with the SLD.
    • Never skip or reorder steps without stopping and getting a revised instruction.
    • After each operation, verify the actual position (mechanical indicator, visual check of the gap) — not just the HMI.
    • Record every operation and time in the station logbook.

    Bus transfer in a double-bus substation

    To move a circuit from Bus 1 to Bus 2 on load:

    1. Close the bus coupler breaker (Bus 1 and Bus 2 now paralleled); make its control local/blocked so it cannot trip during transfer.
    2. Close the circuit's Bus 2 selector disconnector — permitted because both buses are at the same potential through the coupler (make-before-break).
    3. Open the Bus 1 selector disconnector.
    4. Check the bus differential protection follows the new configuration (disconnector replica) and the circuit is now on Bus 2.
    5. Open the bus coupler if required by the operating configuration.

    Transformer operation

    • Energising: expect magnetising inrush up to many times rated current, decaying over seconds — harmonic restraint prevents differential tripping. Energise from the HV side with all protection in service.
    • Paralleling: confirm tap positions match (or are within the allowed step difference), same vector group, and parallel-control mode active (master-follower or circulating-current minimisation).
    • Loading: monitor top-oil and winding temperatures; know the emergency loading limits per the loading guide.
    • Voltage control: AVR in auto keeps bus voltage in band; switch capacitor banks for reactive support before reaching tap limits.

    Safety in Substations

    Substation hazards are invisible and unforgiving. Safety depends on procedures, discipline and never assuming equipment is dead.

    Main hazards

    Electric shock

    Contact with live parts, induced voltages, backfeed, or charged capacitors and cables.

    Arc flash & blast

    Temperatures of thousands of degrees, pressure wave, molten metal, intense light and sound.

    Step & touch potential

    During ground faults, especially near the fence and outside the grid.

    Fire & explosion

    Transformer oil fires, bushing failures, battery hydrogen.

    Mechanical

    Stored energy in breaker springs, hydraulics, moving disconnector arms, heavy lifting.

    Chemical

    Battery acid/alkali, arced SF₆ decomposition products, PCB in old oil.

    Environmental

    Heat stress (very real in tropical climates), lightning storms, snakes and insects, working at height, confined spaces (cable vaults).

    The five golden rules (EN 50110)

    1. Disconnect completely — open breakers and disconnectors to create visible isolation from all sources (including backfeeds and LV side).
    2. Secure against reconnection — lock out, tag out, remove fuses, disable remote control and motors.
    3. Verify absence of voltage — on all phases, at the work location, with a tested voltage detector (test it before and after on a known live source).
    4. Earth and short-circuit — close earth switches and apply portable grounds on all sides of the work location, visible from the work area.
    5. Protect against adjacent live parts — barriers, screens, safety distances, and a clear demarcation of the safe work area.

    Permit-to-work and LOTO

    • A permit-to-work (PTW) or clearance is issued by an authorised person to a competent person in charge of the work, listing isolation points, earth locations, the safe work area and hazards.
    • Lockout/Tagout (LOTO): each worker applies a personal lock; tags show who isolated and why. Locks are only removed by those who applied them.
    • Hold a pre-job briefing (tailboard): scope, hazards, isolation, PPE, emergency plan, roles.
    • Cancel the permit only when all people and tools are clear and all portable grounds removed and accounted for.

    Approach boundaries

    Unqualified persons must stay outside the limited approach boundary; qualified persons may cross the restricted boundary only with proper planning, PPE and insulated tools.     Arc flash incident energy depends on fault current, clearing time and working distance (calculated with IEEE 1584). The onset of a second-degree burn on bare skin is about 1.2 cal/cm². NFPA 70E PPE categories:

    Reducing arc flash hazard: faster protection (arc-flash relays with light sensors, maintenance-mode switches that lower settings during work), remote racking and remote switching, arc-resistant switchgear, and increasing working distance.

    Other safety practices

    • Induced voltage: lines paralleling energised circuits can carry dangerous induced voltage even when isolated — use working grounds at the work site and equipotential zones.
    • Capacitor banks and cables store charge — wait the specified discharge time (typically 5 min), then ground before touching.
    • SF₆ is non-toxic but heavier than air (asphyxiation risk in low spaces); after arcing its by-products are toxic and corrosive — use respirators, gloves and extraction when opening arced compartments.
    • Mobile equipment (cranes, boom trucks) must keep clearances from live parts; use spotters and ground the vehicle.
    • Hot sticks and live-line tools must be clean, dry, inspected and periodically dielectrically tested.
    • Lightning: suspend outdoor yard work when thunderstorms approach.
    • Battery rooms: no smoking or sparks (hydrogen), eyewash available, acid-resistant PPE.

    Emergency response

    Electrical contact rescueDo not touch the victim until the source is de-energised (or use a proper insulated rescue hook where trained). Call for help, de-energise, then start CPR and use an AED if needed. Every substation crew should be trained in CPR and first aid, and the site emergency plan, nearest hospital and emergency numbers should be posted.
    • Transformer fire: de-energise first; use the fixed suppression system; on energised equipment use CO₂ or dry chemical, never water jets. Call the fire service and inform them of the electrical hazard.
    • Oil spill: contain with absorbents, protect drains, report per environmental rules.
    • Explosion / failed bushing: keep clear — secondary failures can follow; establish an exclusion zone.

    Where equipment typically fails

    Transformers

    Bushings, OLTC, winding insulation (often after through-faults or moisture/ageing), leads and connections, cooling systems.

    Circuit breakers

    Operating mechanism and control circuits (majority of major failures), then gas tightness and interrupters.

    Disconnectors

    Contact overheating, seized mechanisms, misalignment, motor drives.

    Instrument transformers

    Oil-paper insulation breakdown (sometimes explosive), moisture ingress, CVT capacitor element failures.

    Secondary systems

    DC earth faults, wiring errors after modifications, relay settings errors, communication failures.

    Know-howA large share of protection misoperations trace back to human factors: wrong settings, incorrect wiring after a modification, or test switches left in the wrong position. Peer-check every setting change and every isolation, and restore test switches using a checklist.

    Modern Trends & the Future Substation

    Substations are changing faster now than in the previous fifty years: digitisation, decarbonisation and the shift to inverter-based renewables.

    Digital substations

    IEC 61850 process bus replaces thousands of copper wires with fibre. Merging units digitise CT/VT signals at the switchgear; GOOSE carries trips. Safer (no open CT hazard in the relay room), smaller panels, faster engineering and testing.

    Centralised / virtualised protection

    Protection and control functions running on substation-hardened servers or virtual machines rather than one relay per bay.

    SF₆-free switchgear

    Vacuum interrupters with “clean air” (synthetic air) or CO₂-based mixtures, and fluoronitrile gas blends with much lower global-warming potential. The EU's revised F-gas Regulation (2024) phases out SF₆ in new switchgear in stages starting in 2026.

    Condition monitoring & analytics

    Online DGA, bushing, PD and breaker monitors feeding asset-health models, digital twins and machine-learning analytics for predictive maintenance.

    Drones & robots

    Automated thermal and visual inspections, robot patrols in GIS halls and yards, and remote switching robots that keep operators away from arc-flash hazards.

    Renewables & BESS

    Collector substations for solar and wind, co-located battery energy storage, grid-forming inverters, and stricter grid code compliance (fault ride-through, reactive capability).

    HVDC & MMC converters

    VSC/MMC converter stations for interconnections, offshore wind and long-distance links; DC circuit breakers for multi-terminal grids.

    Modular & mobile

    Prefabricated E-houses, skid and containerised substations, and mobile substations for resilience after typhoons and disasters.

    Synchrophasors & WAMS

    PMUs giving time-synchronised grid visibility for oscillation detection and system protection schemes.

    Cyber-physical security

    Defence-in-depth networks, secure protocols (IEC 62351), physical hardening against intrusion and ballistic threats.

    Resilience & climate adaptation

    Raised platforms against flooding, typhoon-rated structures, seismic design, and fire-safe ester-filled transformers.

    Formula Sheet, Worked Examples & Rules of Thumb

    Essential formulas

    Three-phase powerS = √3 · V_LL · I P = √3 · V_LL · I · cosφ Q = √3 · V_LL · I · sinφ
    Rated currentI = S / (√3 · V_LL)
    Fault current behind a transformer (infinite source)I_sc = I_rated / Z_pu S_sc (MVA) = S_rated / Z_pu
    Per-unit systemZ_base = kV² / MVA_base Z_pu,new = Z_pu,old × (MVA_new / MVA_old) × (kV_old / kV_new)²
    Symmetrical fault level from source MVAI_sc (kA) = MVA_sc / (√3 · kV)
    Capacitor bank for power factor correctionQ_c = P · (tanφ₁ − tanφ₂)
    Voltage rise due to a capacitor bank (approx.)ΔV % ≈ (Q_c MVAr / S_sc MVA) × 100
    Ground conductor size (IEEE 80, simplified)A_kcmil = I_kA × K_f × √t_c (K_f ≈ 7.00 annealed soft-drawn copper, 7.06 hard-drawn copper) 1 kcmil = 0.5067 mm²
    Transformer efficiencyη = P_out / (P_out + P_core + x² · P_cu,FL) x = per-unit load; η max when x² · P_cu = P_core
    Wenner soil resistivityρ = 2π · a · R

    Worked examples

    1 · Fault current at a 13.8 kV bus

    A 20 MVA, 69/13.8 kV transformer has 8% impedance. Assume an infinite 69 kV source.

    I_rated = 20,000 kVA / (√3 × 13.8 kV) = 836.7 A I_sc = 836.7 / 0.08 = 10,459 A ≈ 10.5 kA S_sc = 20 / 0.08 = 250 MVA

    Real source impedance would lower this slightly. Feeder breakers rated 25 kA have comfortable margin.

    2 · IDMT relay operating time

    Standard inverse relay, pickup 600 A, TMS 0.2, fault current 6000 A.

    M = 6000 / 600 = 10 t = 0.2 × 0.14 / (10^0.02 − 1) = 0.2 × 0.14 / 0.0471 = 0.2 × 2.97 ≈ 0.59 s

    An upstream relay must operate at least about 0.3 s later (≈ 0.9 s) for the same fault to keep coordination.

    3 · Tolerable touch and step voltage (70 kg person)

    Crushed rock ρs = 3000 Ω·m, Cs ≈ 0.7, fault clearing time ts = 0.5 s.

    E_touch = (1000 + 1.5 × 0.7 × 3000) × 0.157 / √0.5 = 4150 × 0.157 / 0.707 ≈ 921 V E_step = (1000 + 6 × 0.7 × 3000) × 0.157 / √0.5 = 13,600 × 0.157 / 0.707 ≈ 3020 V

    The grid design must keep mesh (touch) voltage below ≈ 921 V and step voltage below ≈ 3020 V. Without the rock layer (bare soil), the limits would be far lower.

    4 · Soil resistivity from a Wenner test

    Probe spacing a = 5 m, measured resistance R = 2.4 Ω.

    ρ = 2π × 5 × 2.4 ≈ 75.4 Ω·m
    5 · Capacitor bank sizing

    A 10 MW load at 0.80 power factor must be corrected to 0.95.

    tanφ₁ = tan(cos⁻¹ 0.80) = 0.750 tanφ₂ = tan(cos⁻¹ 0.95) = 0.329 Q_c = 10 × (0.750 − 0.329) ≈ 4.2 MVAr
    6 · Ground grid conductor size

    Ground fault current 25 kA for 0.5 s, soft-drawn copper.

    A = 25 × 7.00 × √0.5 ≈ 123.7 kcmil ≈ 63 mm²

    Many utilities then select a larger standard size (e.g., 95–120 mm² or 4/0 AWG) for mechanical strength, corrosion allowance and future fault growth.

    7 · Distance relay zone reach

    Line impedance 10 Ω (secondary), shortest adjacent line 6 Ω, longest adjacent line 12 Ω.

    Zone 1 = 0.8 × 10 = 8 Ω (instantaneous) Zone 2 = 10 + 0.5 × 6 = 13 Ω (≥ 1.2 × 10 = 12 Ω ✓), ~0.4 s Zone 3 = 10 + 12 × 1.2 ≈ 24.4 Ω, ~1 s (check load encroachment)

    Rules of thumb & field know-how

    Breakers vs disconnectors

    Breaker opens first, closes last. Disconnectors never break load.

    CT and VT

    Never open a CT secondary; never short a VT secondary.

    Insulation resistance

    A rough minimum for many items: 1 MΩ per kV + 1 MΩ — but trend and PI matter more than one reading.

    Temperature

    Correct IR, PF and resistance readings to a reference temperature (usually 20 °C) before comparing.

    Transformer life

    Every ~6 °C of extra hot-spot temperature halves insulation life; moisture and oxygen accelerate ageing.

    DGA

    Any acetylene deserves attention; rising acetylene deserves urgency.

    Arrester placement

    Closer is better. Every metre of lead adds roughly 1 kV per kA/µs of surge front.

    Protection coordination

    ~0.3 s grading margin; Zone 1 ≈ 80%; pickup ≥ 1.2–1.5 × max load.

    Batteries

    Battery life roughly halves per ~8–10 °C above 25 °C. Keep battery rooms cool.

    Thermography

    Scan at load (≥ 40%); compare like phases; ΔT > 15 °C between phases = act now.

    Documentation

    If it isn't recorded, it didn't happen. Keep as-built drawings current after every change.

    Test before trust

    Test the voltage detector on a known live source before and after use.

    AIS
    Air-insulated switchgear/substation — live parts insulated by atmospheric air.

    Transformer with a shared winding section between HV and LV.
    AVR

    Bay
    Set of equipment connecting one circuit to the busbars.
    BDV
    Breakdown voltage of insulating oil.
    BIL / LIWV
    Basic lightning impulse insulation level / lightning impulse withstand voltage.

    Gas and oil-surge relay between transformer tank and conservator.

    Load connected to an instrument transformer secondary, in VA or ohms.
    Busbar
    Common conductor to which several circuits connect.
    CB
    Circuit breaker — switching device that can make and break normal and fault currents.

    Oil expansion tank on top of a transformer.

    Shortest distance along an insulator surface between conductive parts.
    CT / VT / CVT
    Current transformer / voltage transformer / capacitor voltage transformer.
    CTI
    Coordination time interval between successive protection devices.
    DGA
    Dissolved gas analysis of transformer oil.
    Diameter
    One bay of a breaker-and-a-half scheme (three breakers, two circuits).
    Disconnector / isolator
    Switch providing a visible isolating gap; not for load interruption.

    Switch that connects isolated conductors to earth.
    FAT / SAT
    Factory acceptance test / site acceptance test.

    Maximum short-circuit current (kA) or power (MVA) at a point.
    Ferranti effect
    Voltage rise at the receiving end of a lightly loaded long line or cable.
    Ferroresonance
    Nonlinear resonance between capacitance and a saturable inductance, causing overvoltages.
    GIS
    Gas-insulated switchgear in grounded, gas-filled metal enclosures.
    GOOSE
    IEC 61850 fast peer-to-peer event messaging between IEDs.
    GPR
    Ground potential rise — grid voltage relative to remote earth during a fault.
    IDMT
    Inverse definite minimum time overcurrent characteristic.
    IED
    Intelligent electronic device — numerical relay, controller or meter.
    Inrush current
    Transient magnetising current when a transformer is energised.
    Interlock
    Mechanical, electrical or software logic preventing unsafe operations.
    Knee-point voltage
    CT excitation voltage above which the core saturates rapidly.
    LOTO
    Lockout / tagout isolation procedure.
    Merging unit
    Device digitising CT/VT signals into IEC 61850 Sampled Values.
    MOV / MCOV
    Metal-oxide varistor arrester / maximum continuous operating voltage.
    N-1
    Planning criterion: system remains secure after loss of any single element.
    NGR
    Neutral grounding resistor.
    OLTC / DETC
    On-load tap changer / de-energised tap changer.
    ONAN, ONAF, OFAF, ODAF
    Transformer cooling classes (see Chapter 4).
    PI
    Polarisation index = IR at 10 min ÷ IR at 1 min.
    PLCC
    Power line carrier communication over HV lines.
    Process bus
    IEC 61850 network carrying sampled values and commands between switchyard and IEDs.
    PTW
    Permit to work.
    Recloser
    Self-controlled breaker that automatically recloses after tripping.
    REF
    Restricted earth fault protection for transformer windings.
    RTU
    Remote terminal unit for SCADA.
    SCADA
    Supervisory control and data acquisition.
    SCD / ICD / CID
    IEC 61850 configuration files (substation / IED capability / configured IED).

    Sulphur hexafluoride insulating and arc-quenching gas.
    SFRA
    Sweep frequency response analysis for winding deformation.
    SLD
    Single-line diagram.
    STATCOM / SVC
    Static synchronous compensator / static var compensator.

    Voltage between feet / between hand and feet during a ground fault.

    Transient recovery voltage across breaker contacts after interruption.

    Transformer turns ratio test.

    Self-Check Quiz

    Click an answer to check it. Your score updates as you go.

    Score: 0 / 0

    1. When de-energising a bay, what is operated first?

    The breaker interrupts the load current. Disconnectors are opened only after the breaker is open, because they cannot interrupt load.

    2. Which bus scheme uses three breakers for every two circuits?

    Breaker-and-a-half: each diameter has three breakers in series between two buses, serving two circuits.

    3. What happens if a CT secondary is open-circuited while primary current flows?

    With no secondary current to oppose it, all primary ampere-turns magnetise the core, producing very high peak voltages. Always short CT secondaries before disconnecting.

    4. Which dissolved gas most strongly indicates arcing inside a transformer?

    Acetylene (C₂H₂) forms at very high temperatures typical of arcing; any rising acetylene is serious.

    5. In vector group Dyn11, what does “11” mean?

    The clock number × 30° gives the phase displacement of LV relative to HV: position 11 = 330° lag, i.e., 30° lead.

    6. Typical Zone 1 reach of a distance relay is:

    Zone 1 underreaches to avoid tripping instantaneously for faults beyond the remote bus, allowing for measurement errors.

    7. The Buchholz relay is located:

    It collects gas rising toward the conservator (alarm) and detects oil surges from internal faults (trip).

    8. Which condition is NOT required for paralleling two transformers?

    Ratio, phase displacement, polarity/phase sequence and similar impedance matter — not the manufacturer.

    9. Polarisation index (PI) is the ratio of insulation resistance at:

    PI = IR(10 min) / IR(1 min). A PI below about 1–2 suggests moisture or contamination.

    10. What is the main purpose of crushed rock on a substation yard surface?

    The high-resistivity surface layer adds resistance in series with a person's feet, greatly increasing the tolerable touch and step voltages (it also helps drainage and weed control).

    11. Which relay function blocks transformer differential tripping during energisation?

    Inrush current is rich in second harmonic; 5th-harmonic restraint is for overexcitation.

    12. Which IEC 61850 service carries fast trip and interlock messages between IEDs?

    GOOSE is a fast multicast peer-to-peer message used to replace hard-wired signals.

    13. A 30 MVA transformer has 10% impedance. Ignoring source impedance, its maximum through-fault MVA is:

    S_sc = S_rated / Z_pu = 30 / 0.10 = 300 MVA.

    14. Which of the five golden rules comes immediately after “secure against reconnection”?

    Disconnect → secure → verify absence of voltage → earth and short-circuit → protect against adjacent live parts.

    15. In thermography, a ΔT of 20 °C between similar phase connections under similar load means:

    Per the NETA table, ΔT greater than 15 °C between similar components indicates a major discrepancy requiring immediate repair.

    16. Why is substation DC usually ungrounded (floating) with earth-fault monitoring?

    A floating system tolerates one earth fault; the monitor alarms so it can be fixed before a second fault causes a false trip or failure to trip.

    17. Which breaker interrupting medium dominates MV switchgear today?

    Vacuum interrupters dominate up to about 40.5 kV; SF₆ dominates at HV/EHV, though vacuum is expanding upward.

    18. The wave trap (line trap) is connected:

    It is a parallel LC circuit in series with the line, blocking carrier frequencies while passing power frequency.

    19. Where should surge arresters protecting a transformer be installed?

    Lead length and separation distance raise the voltage the transformer sees, so arresters go as close as possible.

    20. What does breaker failure protection (50BF) do?

    If current persists after a trip command plus a short delay, 50BF trips the surrounding breakers (and sends a transfer trip to remote ends).
